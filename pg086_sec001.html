The shape controls how sound waves travel, influencing the tone. For example, a trumpet’s narrow tube creates a bright sound, while a tuba’s wide shape produces a deep tone. The size determines pitch, with larger instruments (such as a bass drum) producing lower pitches and smaller ones (such as a snare drum) creating higher pitches. The material affects timbre or tone colour of an instrument, for example a wooden guitar compared to a metal trumpet.

(b) The harmonic theory:

This theory explains why different instruments sound unique, even when playing the same note. According to the theory, every musical sound consists of a fundamental tone (the main pitch we hear) and overtones (extra frequencies that shape the sound). These overtones create harmonics, which give each instrument a distinct timbre. For example, a piano and a violin playing the same note will sound different because of their unique overtones. The material and structure of an instrument influence how these harmonics are produced. This theory helps explain why different musical instruments can be recognised instantly, even when they play the same melody.

(c) The mathematical theory:

This theory suggests that musical sounds follow mathematical patterns. The Greek philosopher known as Pythagoras discovered that the length of a vibrating string determines its pitch. For example, when a guitar string is cut in half, it produces a pitch one octave higher. This theory is also considered when tuning instruments, particularly in Western music. For example, by considering the mathematical relationships between tones, an octave is divided into 12 equal steps.

(d) The biological theory:

This theory explains that the human brain is wired to detect music. Studies show that the brain processes musical sounds in a special way, different from other sounds. It helps us recognise melodies, rhythms and harmonies, and some people even have perfect pitch, meaning they can identify musical notes without any reference from a musical instrument. Studies also show that music affects emotions and memory. For example, babies respond to lullabies, proving that musical sounds influence human emotions from birth. Scientists have discovered that listening to music releases dopamine, a chemical that makes us feel happy. This explains why people enjoy music, feel emotional connections to songs and use music for relaxation or motivation.
